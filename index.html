    How Rally Lens measures, and how to film for it

    Ball tracking

    Every frame is compared with the frames either side of it to find small moving blobs, and only optic-yellow blobs are kept. They are linked into flight paths that obey a ball's maximum speed. Players, shadows and racquets drop out because they are the wrong size or colour.

    Speed and spin

    Your four corner clicks give the camera's position, angle and zoom. Each flight between a hit and its bounce is then fitted with a 3D model that includes gravity, air drag and Magnus lift. Speed is the fitted launch speed, extrapolated back to the contact point. Spin is the topspin or backspin needed to bend the path that much, from the lift curve of a tennis ball.

    Bounce

    The bounce is where the fitted path meets the court, so it is placed in metres and called in or out against the lines you marked. The flight after the bounce is fitted separately to get the speed off the court and the ball's height as it reaches the baseline (kick).

    Shot quality, 0 to 10

    Modelled on ATP Shot Quality, which rates serve, return, forehand and backhand from speed, spin, depth, width and impact on the opponent. Rally shots here weigh speed 30%, depth 25%, heaviness after the bounce 20%, spin 15% and width 10%. Serves weigh speed 55%, placement near the T or wide corners 30% and spin 15%. A ball that lands out scores 0. ATP's version is a win-probability model trained on pro tracking data, so treat comparisons with tour averages as rough.

    Filming checklist

    • Tripod or fence mount. A hand-held camera moves the whole picture and breaks tracking.
    • Behind a baseline, raised 3 m or more if you can, with all four corners and every player head to toe in shot.
    • 60 fps or more. 30 fps works, but spin and post-bounce figures get less certain.
    • Landscape, good light, a fresh yellow ball. Export iPhone HEVC as H.264 if your browser won't play the file.

    Coach's notes and highlights

    Notes come from patterns in the measured shots: first-serve percentage, double faults, depth, net margin, spin, direction, winners against errors, returns, net play and movement. Each note links to the shots behind it. Highlights pick out aces, winners, the fastest and best-rated shots, close calls, volleys and long rallies. Play them as a reel, or record the reel with its overlays as a video to share. Copy report puts a text summary on your clipboard for your coach.

    Scoring and match stats

    A point starts with a serve. A serve that lands out is a fault, and the next serve is the second serve. A point ends when a ball lands out (an error), bounces without a reply (a winner, or an ace off the serve), or a reply is struck but fails. Errors count as forced when the incoming ball rated 6.5 or higher. Points the clip cuts off are marked unfinished and are not scored. Set the games already played if the clip starts mid-set. Movement maps and distances come from the player tracking.

    Volleys and overheads

    When a flight ends on a racket rather than the court, the shot is marked as taken in the air. With no bounce to anchor it, its path is pinned by gravity and by where the receiving player is standing. The reply is labelled a volley, or an overhead when struck above 2.25 m. It is scored on speed, width and depth, with reaction time and the incoming ball's speed shown alongside. Speed and spin on a ball taken in the air are less certain than on one that bounces.

    Players and doubles

    Players are found as moving figures against a background built from frames across the clip, then followed through the rally by position and shirt colour. Each shot goes to the player on the hitting side nearest the contact point, which is how partners in doubles are told apart. Name everyone in step 1; left and right are as seen from the camera.

    Finding the court

    When a video loads, a few frames spread across it are blended so moving players vanish. Bright, thin, low-colour stripes are picked out and turned into straight lines. Every way of matching them to a real court (baselines, service lines, sidelines and the net tape) is tried, and the match that explains the most line paint wins. That gives the camera's position and angle with nothing to click, from either end, off-centre, high or low, and copes with faded, shadowed or partly covered lines. If too little matches, you're asked to check it or mark the corners yourself. If the camera moves, every frame is shifted back onto the first one before tracking.

    Line calls

    Each bounce is placed twice: from the fitted 3D flight, and from the sharp change of direction in the picture. The two are combined, and the leftover uncertainty (fit, frame rate, blur, and how well the court was located) becomes an error ellipse on the ground. A ball touching any part of the line is in. The chance it touched is the visual verdict. It is then weighed with other evidence: did the receiver play it or stop, did they shout, the racket sound of a reply, and (with the optional AI helpers) a raised finger, a palm-down signal or the words "out" or "in". Clear visual evidence counts most, reactions can only tip a borderline ball, and disagreements are flagged. A call is "confirmed" at 95% or more and "likely" at 85% or more. Below that it is shown as too close to call, and the point is left unscored rather than guessed.

    What it can't see yet

    Sidespin, forehand versus backhand, footwork and decision-making all need body-pose tracking, which is the next stage. Hand signals and spoken calls need the optional AI helpers, which download about 45 MB on first use and need an internet connection; they may be blocked inside some embedded previews. Rows marked "low" confidence had few clean frames. Your video never leaves this browser.
